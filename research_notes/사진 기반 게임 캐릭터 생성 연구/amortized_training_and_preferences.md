## 1. How did face-to-parameter / avatar works train fast predictors, and what closed the synthetic-to-real gap?

### Takeaway
The game-character literature uses three ways to get a fast predictor. (a) Self-supervised training through a differentiable "imitator" of the game engine with face-identity losses on unlabeled real photos (NetEase F2P → Fast-F2P). (b) Synthetic paired data that bridges the domains by making renders look real (SwiftAvatar), or by making photos look like renders before regression (AgileAvatar). (c) Domain-invariant intermediate representations. Outside games, Microsoft (Fake-It, DigiFace-1M) and MPI (BEDLAM) show that large, diverse, heavily augmented synthetic sets can match real-data training, and that a small real set for fine-tuning closes most of the remaining gap.

### Cited Findings
**NetEase / game face-to-parameter line**
- F2P (ICCV 2019, Shi, Yuan et al.) treats character creation as optimisation over "a large set of physically meaningful facial parameters". An **Imitator G(x)** (DC-GAN-like, eight transposed-conv layers) mimics the game engine (parameters → rendered face), and a feature extractor defines facial similarity. Parameters are optimised by gradient descent with a "discriminative loss" and a "facial content loss" in a neural-style-transfer framework — [ICCV 2019 open access](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ICCV_2019/html/Shi_Face-to-Parameter_Translation_for_Game_Character_Auto-Creation_ICCV_2019_paper.html); [arXiv 1909.01064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.01064)
- Fast and Robust F2P (AAAI 2020, Shi, Zou, Yuan, Fan) adds a **facial parameter translator** that maps face embeddings to parameters in a single forward pass. It is trained **self-supervised**, with an identity loss, a facial content loss and a **loopback loss** ("the translator correctly interprets its own output"). It needs "neither ground truth references nor user interactions", gives about a **1000× speed-up** over iterative F2P, and is more robust to head pose. Users can still hand-tune the output parameters — [AAAI page](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5537); [arXiv 2008.07132](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.07132)
- The journal extension "Neural Rendering for Game Character Auto-creation" (Shi et al., 2022) keeps the imitator + self-supervised paradigm. It was "applied to two games" and "has provided over 10 million times of online services" — [BUAA PDF](https://levir.buaa.edu.cn/static/pdfs/2022_tianyang_shi_neural.pdf) (venue is TPAMI per my recollection; the search snippet did not confirm it)
- In NetEase's text-to-parameter follow-up, the game's facial parameters include **208 continuous controllers plus discrete parameters** for hairstyles, eyebrow styles, lipstick styles and colours. That is a mixed continuous/discrete setting like CK3's — [DeepAI: Zero-Shot T2P](https://deepai.org/publication/zero-shot-text-to-parameter-translation-for-game-character-auto-creation). A 2025 real-time T2P variant also exists — [Neurocomputing 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231225004515) **(2024–2026)**. A related dialogue-based editor is ICE (2024) — [arXiv 2403.12667](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.12667) **(2024–2026)**
- MeInGame (AAAI 2021, NetEase Fuxi + U. Michigan) takes a different route: 3DMM (BFM09) reconstruction plus shape transfer to the game mesh, plus texture. Its texture dataset was built from CelebA-HQ in six subsets ({Caucasian, Asian, African} × {female, male}), 400 texture maps each, split 300/50/50 for train/val/test. That is an explicitly demographically balanced design. Code and data: [github.com/FuxiCV/MeInGame](https://github.com/FuxiCV/MeInGame) — [arXiv 2102.02371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02371); [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/16106)

**Stylised-avatar (Bitmoji-like) line**
- SwiftAvatar (AAAI 2023) has three parts: **dual-domain generators**, a **cross-domain paired-data pipeline**, and a **light-weight avatar estimator**. Both generators use SemanticStyleGAN. The realistic generator is pretrained on real faces. The avatar generator is transfer-learned on engine renders with colour-consistency constraints, so the same latent gives a similar-looking real face and avatar. Data generation: **randomly sample avatar vectors → render in the engine → invert the render into the avatar generator's latent → decode that latent with the realistic generator** to get a photo-like face paired with the known vector. The estimator is trained on these synthetic pairs and predicts avatar vectors directly from a selfie. It was shown on two avatar engines, and in a 50-volunteer human study it scored above two recent methods — [AAAI PDF](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/25753/25525); [arXiv 2301.08153](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08153); [Underline talk](https://ai.underline.io/lecture/67801-swiftavatar-efficient-auto-creation-of-parameterized-stylized-character-on-arbitrary-avatar-engines)
- AgileAvatar (SIGGRAPH Asia 2022, ByteDance) predicts an avatar vector of **continuous (e.g. head length) and discrete (e.g. hair type)** parameters. It argues that supervised annotation "requires large scale annotations to handle a large range of assets (usually in the hundreds)". Its pipeline is cascaded domain bridging: (1) a modified portrait-stylisation network translates the selfie into an avatar-style image, (2) a **differentiable imitator** of the engine lets continuous parameters be optimised, and (3) a **cascaded relaxation-and-search** handles discrete parameters. In the search step, each attribute's full asset list is enumerated while all other parameters stay fixed, scoring against the imitator image. In a human preference study it beat prior work and came "close to those of manual creation" — [arXiv 2211.07818](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.07818); [SIGGRAPH history](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=215758)
- Snapmoji (WACV 2026, Snap) maps a selfie to a Bitmoji-style primary avatar with "Gaussian Domain Adaptation" in **0.9 s**, then restyles it. Animation runs at 30–40 FPS on mobile **(2024–2026)** — [WACV 2026 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2026/papers/Chen_Snapmoji_Instant_Generation_of_Animatable_Dual-Stylized_Avatars_WACV_2026_paper.pdf); [arXiv 2503.11978](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11978v2). It does not regress engine parameters, so it is only loosely relevant.

**Synthetic-only / synthetic-plus-few-real evidence (faces and bodies)**
- "Fake It Till You Make It" (Wood et al., ICCV 2021, Microsoft) combines a procedurally generated parametric 3D face with a large library of hand-crafted assets. Landmark localisation and face parsing trained on **synthetic data alone** "match real data in accuracy". The authors note that earlier attempts to bridge the gap used data mixing, domain adaptation and domain-adversarial training — [ICCV 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/html/Wood_Fake_It_Till_You_Make_It_Face_Analysis_in_the_ICCV_2021_paper.html); [MSR page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=780655)
- DigiFace-1M (WACV 2023) contains 1.22M synthetic images of 110K identities. **"Aggressive data augmentation can significantly reduce the synthetic-to-real domain gap."** Fine-tuning on only **120K real images (2% of MS1MV2)** gives 99.33% on LFW and 93.61% averaged over five benchmarks, "comparable to methods trained on millions of real face images" — [WACV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2023/html/Bae_DigiFace-1M_1_Million_Digital_Face_Images_for_Face_Recognition_WACV_2023_paper.html); [arXiv 2210.02579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.02579)
- BEDLAM (CVPR 2023) shows that human pose/shape regressors trained **only on synthetic data** reach SOTA on real benchmarks. "With good synthetic training data, a basic method like HMR approaches the accuracy of the current SOTA method (CLIFF)." BEDLAM 2.0 has more than 8M images — [CVPR 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2023/html/Black_BEDLAM_A_Synthetic_Dataset_of_Bodies_Exhibiting_Detailed_Lifelike_Animated_CVPR_2023_paper.html); [BEDLAM2](https://bedlam2.is.tue.mpg.de/) **(2024–2026 for v2)**
- SMIRK (CVPR 2024) found two weaknesses in self-supervised 3D face regressors: the self-supervised loss formulation (photometric rendering) and too little expression diversity in training images. It replaces the differentiable renderer with a **neural renderer** in the analysis-by-synthesis loop **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Retsinas_3D_Facial_Expressions_through_Analysis-by-Neural-Synthesis_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf); [MPI page](https://is.mpg.de/publications/retsinas2024smirk)

### Inferences
- **CK3 is closest to SwiftAvatar or AgileAvatar.** Both are stylised engines with mixed continuous and discrete parameters, not photoreal 3DMMs. The domain gap is mainly a **style gap**: a CK3 render of person X never looks like a photo of X. So naive "train on renders, test on photos" with a pixel CNN will struggle more than Fake-It/BEDLAM, whose renders aim at photorealism.
- The cheapest way to bridge the gap, and the one the existing 3-minute pipeline already uses implicitly, is a **shared intermediate representation**. Run the same frozen extractors (dense landmarks / landmark-derived geometric ratios, face-parsing masks, a face-ID embedding, a CLIP or DINO-type embedding) on both renders and photos, then regress DNA from those features. Geometry ratios transfer between domains much better than pixels. ID and CLIP embeddings transfer only partly, because renders sit in a different region of embedding space; that offset is what the small expert set and the teacher labels can calibrate (§3).
- **Data volume.** At about 1000 renders/min the factory makes about 60k renders/h, so 300k–1M renders × 4 cameras is a single-night job. DigiFace (1.2M) and BEDLAM (1M+, 2.0 at 8M+) suggest that range for synthetic-only success. However, if inputs are frozen-feature vectors and not pixels, ~100–300k DNA samples is probably plenty for ~100 outputs. This is my judgement; there is no direct source.
- **Augmentation** (DigiFace's lesson): apply aggressive photometric and geometric augmentation to renders (colour jitter, blur, JPEG, noise, random crops/scale/rotation, background replacement, camera randomisation across the 4+ cameras). Randomise the *non-identity* genes (expression, age-neutral variables, clothing, lighting if exposed) so the model learns invariance to them.
- **DNA sampling distribution for synthetic data matters.** The regressor learns a posterior mean under the sampling prior. Use a mixture: about half drawn from a prior fitted to human presets and vanilla characters (realistic region), about half broad or uniform (coverage of extremes). Otherwise the model either never predicts unusual faces or produces implausible ones.
- **Optional SwiftAvatar-style bridge on a single GPU:** a StyleGAN/FFHQ generator plus a fine-tuned "CK3-render generator" sharing a latent space is feasible but heavy for a hobby project. Treat it as phase-3, after the feature-space approach is benchmarked. The non-commercial FFHQ licence is fine for local use.
- **Fast-F2P-style self-supervision** is the other main option. Train the predictor on unlabeled FFHQ photos by passing its DNA through a differentiable surrogate of the engine and maximising photo-to-surrogate-render identity similarity, plus a loopback/cycle loss (render → predictor → same DNA). It needs an imitator. With 24k+ renders, a **DNA → render-features surrogate** (predicting the ID embedding, landmarks and CLIP embedding a render would produce) is much easier to train than a full DNA → image imitator, and supports the same losses.

- Could not confirm F2P's exact parameter count, training-set sizes or Fast-F2P's training photo set (I believe it is CelebA-based; unverified).

## 2. Distilling a slow test-time optimiser into a feed-forward network

### Takeaway
The best-documented recipe comes from human-mesh recovery. Regress first and use the regression as an optimiser initialisation, use optimiser results (filtered) as pseudo-labels, and repeat for a few rounds (SPIN → EFT → CameraHMR). Add cheap iterative refinement inside the network (HMR's IEF, PyMAF's alignment feedback) or a learned optimiser (LGD, Learning-to-Fit). Expect a 100–1000× speed-up for feed-forward prediction (Fast-F2P reports about 1000×), with refinement adding tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

### Cited Findings
- **Amortised-optimisation taxonomy** (Amos, Foundations & Trends in ML 2023): models are **fully amortised** (predict the solution directly) or **semi-amortised** (predict, then run some optimisation steps). Learning is **regression-based** (regress onto optimiser solutions) or **objective-based** (backpropagate the optimisation objective itself). Warm-start + fine-tune "only learns the initial prediction", while semi-amortised learning "end-to-end learns through the final prediction". Code: facebookresearch/amortized-optimization-tutorial — [arXiv 2202.00665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.00665); [NOW Publishers](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/MAL-102)
- **Iterative Error Feedback** (Carreira et al.): at each iteration a function predicts a correction that improves the current output. It is trained on bounded corrections across several steps — [arXiv 1507.06550](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.06550). HMR's IEF loop "uses the same global features each time", which "lacks fine-grained information". **PyMAF** instead re-extracts **mesh-aligned features** from a feature pyramid at the current estimate and feeds them back to rectify parameters — [arXiv 2103.16507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.16507)
- **SPIN** ("model-fitting in the loop", ICCV 2019): "a reasonable regressed estimate … initializes properly the optimization, thus leading to a better optimum. Similarly, a value optimized by iterative fitting can act as supervision to better train the network" — [arXiv 1909.12828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.12828)
- **EFT** (Joo, Neverova, Vedaldi; 3DV 2021) fine-tunes a pretrained regressor *per exemplar* to fit 2D keypoints. This uses the regressor's implicit prior instead of a generic prior. Pseudo-GT from EFT on COCO/MPII "can supervise a strong 3D pose estimator that outperforms the previous state-of-the-art" on 3DPW **without any ground-truth 3D datasets** — [arXiv 2004.03686](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.03686); [3DV 2021](https://3dv2021.surrey.ac.uk/papers/374.html)
- **CameraHMR** (3DV 2025): v1 is trained on synthetic BEDLAM. It then initialises SMPLify fits on 4M real crops; filtering keeps about **2.8M of 4M**; v2 is trained on the result. "The whole process runs for multiple iterations of refinement" **(2024–2026)** — [arXiv 2411.08128](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.08128); [project](https://camerahmr.is.tue.mpg.de)
- **Learned Gradient Descent** (Song, Chen, Hilliges, ECCV 2020): a network predicts the per-iteration parameter update. It converges in "very few steps", about **120 ms** on average, is trained only from MoCap SMPL data, and improves on SMPLify by **45%** on 3DPW — [ECCV 2020](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2020/papers_ECCV/html/3610_ECCV_2020_paper.php); [arXiv 2008.08474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08474v1)
- **Learning to Fit Morphable Models** (Choutas, Bogo, Shen, Valentin; ECCV 2022) uses a Levenberg–Marquardt-inspired learned update. It is demonstrated on face-surface estimation from **dense 2D landmarks** (and bodies) and is "competitive … both in terms of accuracy and speed" with tuned classical fitting — [ECVA](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2022/papers_ECCV/html/4927_ECCV_2022_paper.php); [arXiv 2111.14824](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.14824)
- **ScoreHMR** (CVPR 2024) trains a diffusion model over body-model parameters conditioned on the image. At test time it guides denoising with a task-specific score (e.g. keypoint reprojection), giving a learned-prior optimiser that "solve[s] inverse problems … without needing to retrain" **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Stathopoulos_Score-Guided_Diffusion_for_3D_Human_Recovery_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)
- Fast-F2P's ~1000× speed-up over iterative F2P is the game-domain reference point for the speed of a fully amortised predictor — [AAAI 2020](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5537)

### Inferences
- **Teacher cost reality check.** 3 min/person × 70k FFHQ = 210,000 min ≈ **3,500 GPU-hours (~146 days)**, so the teacher cannot label all of FFHQ. Options: (a) label a **diverse, demographically stratified subset** (e.g. 3–5k photos ≈ 150–250 h ≈ 1–1.5 weeks of overnight runs); (b) **SPIN/CameraHMR loop**: once a v1 student exists, use it to warm-start the teacher's optimiser with far fewer iterations (if the optimisation dominates the 3 min, that could cut cost 3–10×; not measured), then re-label more photos.
- **Teacher labels as weighted soft targets, not ground truth.** Judge score is about 4–5/10, so pure regression onto the teacher caps quality near the teacher and inherits its biases. EFT/SPIN suggest students can match or slightly beat noisy teachers by averaging out per-sample optimiser noise. Follow CameraHMR and **filter** teacher outputs by a quality signal (landmark residual, ID similarity of the rendered result, reward-model score). Weight samples by confidence.
- **Recommended staged training** (curriculum by label quality):
  1. Pretrain on synthetic renders (exact DNA labels, ~300k+) using feature-space inputs.
  2. Add teacher-labelled real photos (thousands, filtered and weighted).
  3. Fine-tune or residual-correct with the ~150–270 expert pairs (§3).
  4. Optional preference-based improvement (§4).
- **Refinement within the 5-second budget.** The engine renders about 1000/min ≈ 17/s (if a warm render server can be called synchronously; to verify). A feed-forward pass (~10–50 ms) leaves room for **best-of-N with real renders**: sample 16–48 candidate DNAs (from mixture or hypothesis heads, §5), render all, score each against the photo (ID similarity + landmark-geometry distance + reward model), and return the best. This is "render-and-compare" without differentiable rendering, and fits 5 s with N ≈ 30–60. A **differentiable surrogate** (DNA → predicted landmarks/ID/CLIP features of the render, trained on the 24k+ renders) allows 20–100 gradient steps in milliseconds (LGD/Learning-to-Fit style), before the real-render re-rank.
- **IEF inside the network** is nearly free: feed (photo features, current DNA estimate, surrogate features of the current DNA) back for 2–3 iterations. Training it requires the surrogate (PyMAF lesson: feedback must include *current-estimate-aligned* evidence, not just the same global features).
- **Objective-based amortisation** (Amos) is also possible. Train the student directly on the teacher's *loss* (landmark/ID/CLIP objectives through the surrogate) on unlabeled FFHQ instead of on teacher outputs. This is essentially Fast-F2P and avoids the teacher's 3-minute cost, but needs a good surrogate and loss weighting. A hybrid loss (teacher-label regression + objective term) is the semi-amortised sweet spot.

### Gaps
- No published speed/quality trade-off curve exists for "feed-forward vs feed-forward + k refinement steps" in a game-parameter setting. In HMR work refinement generally improves alignment, but I found no CK3-like numbers.
- SPIN's exact quantitative gains and HMR-2.0/4DHumans pseudo-label volumes were not retrieved.

## 3. Exploiting ~150–270 human-made photo→parameter pairs (and multiple presets per person)

### Takeaway
With about 143 identities, the expert set is too small to train on directly. It is best used (1) as the **identity-grouped held-out benchmark**, (2) for **low-capacity residual or calibration models** on top of the synthetic- and teacher-trained student, (3) as the basis of a **plausibility prior** (PCA/GMM/VAE over preset DNA, like SMPLify's GMM or VPoser), and (4) via inter-preset disagreement, as **per-gene uncertainty weights**. DigiFace's "small real fine-tune on large synthetic pretraining" shows the pattern works, at a much larger real-data scale.

### Cited Findings
- **Large synthetic pretraining + small real fine-tune:** fine-tuning on 2% of a standard real dataset made synthetic-pretrained face recognition comparable to full real training — [DigiFace-1M, WACV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2023/html/Bae_DigiFace-1M_1_Million_Digital_Face_Images_for_Face_Recognition_WACV_2023_paper.html)
- **Priors from human-made parameter sets:** SMPLify fits a **mixture of Gaussians to ~1M MoCap poses** and uses its negative log as a plausibility prior. SMPL-X/SMPLify-X replaced it with **VPoser**, a VAE prior, because the GMM "is not sufficiently strong" — [SMPL-X / Expressive Body Capture, arXiv 1904.05866](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.05866); [EmergentMind summary of SMPLify](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/smplify)
- **Annotator disagreement as uncertainty:** disagreement among expert annotators reflects irreducible ambiguity (aleatoric uncertainty). Heteroscedastic regression predicts a per-sample variance alongside the value, and soft labels or keeping all annotations retains this information. "Data perspectivism" treats annotations as opinions, not gold facts — [Disentangling Uncertainty in MT Evaluation, arXiv 2204.06546](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.06546); [Expert-guided uncertainty modelling, arXiv 2604.01898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01898) **(2024–2026)**; [Learning annotator disagreement, arXiv 2305.01050](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.01050)
- **Balanced small sets by design:** MeInGame's dataset used equal 400-sample subsets per ethnicity × gender — [arXiv 2102.02371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02371)
- **Supervised annotation does not scale across many assets** (AgileAvatar's motivation for self-supervision), which supports using the human pairs for calibration and evaluation, not as the main training signal — [arXiv 2211.07818](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.07818)

### Inferences
- **Effective N is the number of identities (about 143), not the number of photos.** All splits must be **GroupKFold by identity**, with photos and presets of one person on the same side. Otherwise residual models memorise faces. Reserve about 30–40 identities (stratified by sex, age band, ethnicity and preset quality) as a frozen test set; never tune on it.
- **Residual correction (recommended first use):** fit `Δ = DNA_expert − DNA_student` as a function of input features using **ridge regression / PLS / a Gaussian process with a linear-plus-RBF kernel**, or a tiny MLP with heavy weight decay. Shrink toward zero by cross-validated regularisation. Because Δ is mostly systematic (render-domain offsets, e.g. "the student under-predicts jaw width"), a per-gene **linear calibration** (`a_g · pred_g + b_g`, partially pooled across genes in a hierarchical/empirical-Bayes way) often captures most of the gain with about 2 parameters per gene.
- **Variance/"caricature" calibration:** L2-trained students shrink toward the mean (see §6). Use the expert set to fit per-gene slope > 1 corrections that restore the right spread of deviations from the population mean. This is a direct anti-"averageness" fix.
- **Few-shot fine-tuning:** if fine-tuning the network itself, freeze the encoder. Fine-tune only the head (or LoRA adapters), mix expert pairs into every batch with up-weighting (importance weighting by community quality label, e.g. weight ∝ quality score), and keep an L2-SP / KL penalty to the synthetic-pretrained weights. Choose the epoch count by identity-grouped CV.
- **Plausibility prior:** fit PCA, then a GMM (K ≈ 3–8) or a small VAE over **all available human-made DNA**. That includes community presets *without* photos and vanilla CK3 historical characters, which may number in the thousands even if photo-paired ones are few. Use its negative log-likelihood as (a) a regulariser in refinement, (b) a filter on teacher pseudo-labels, and (c) a re-ranking term in best-of-N. Caution: a prior from celebrity presets encodes celebrity/beautification bias. Prefer vanilla-character DNA plus presets, and weight the prior lightly.
- **Multiple presets per person (inter-annotator variability):**
  - Compute per-gene spread across presets of the same person. Genes with large spread are ambiguous or style-driven; down-weight them in the loss (heteroscedastic / inverse-variance weighting) or let a heteroscedastic head learn them.
  - Use the spread as the **"human-level" bar**: a model whose error on a gene falls inside inter-preset spread is as good as another preset maker.
  - Treat the different presets as **several valid answers** (multi-modality). Train with a **min-over-targets** loss (distance to the nearest preset) or a mixture-likelihood loss, not the mean of presets, which would itself be averaged and "beautified".
- **Combining 2–3 photos per person:** use them as augmentation (same DNA target, different photos) and to measure **prediction consistency across photos** (an unsupervised quality metric applicable to FFHQ-like data too: the same person should yield similar DNA).
- **Vanilla-only (~175) vs modded presets:** train only on vanilla-gene presets. Modded genes have no render-factory counterpart.

## 4. Preference learning for parameter generators (reward models, best-of-N, reward-weighted regression, DPO-style fine-tuning) and guarding against reward hacking

### Takeaway
With a few thousand AI-judge votes, the most robust plan is a **small Bradley–Terry reward ensemble on frozen features**. Use it for **conservative best-of-N re-ranking** at inference and for **best-of-N or reward-weighted distillation** back into the student (RAFT/AWR/BOND-like). DPO-style fine-tuning works if the generator has a tractable likelihood (MDN/flow) or is a diffusion model (Diffusion-DPO). Over-optimisation is the main risk. Mitigate it with ensembles (worst-case or uncertainty-weighted scoring), moderate N, an anchor or KL to the reference model, and a human-checked hold-out.

### Cited Findings
- **Diffusion-DPO** (Wallace et al., CVPR 2024) reformulates DPO for diffusion by using the ELBO as the likelihood. Trained on **851K** Pick-a-Pic pairwise preferences, the fine-tuned SDXL base beat SDXL base and SDXL+refiner in human evaluation. An **AI-feedback variant performed comparably to human preferences** **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/html/Wallace_Diffusion_Model_Alignment_Using_Direct_Preference_Optimization_CVPR_2024_paper.html)
- **ImageReward / ReFL** (NeurIPS 2023): a reward model trained on **137k expert comparisons**, plus ReFL, which "directly tunes" diffusion models against the scorer — [NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/33646ef0ed554145eab65f6250fab0c9-Abstract.html)
- **Reward over-optimisation** (Gao, Schulman, Hilton; ICML 2023): optimising a proxy reward model eventually hurts the "gold" reward (Goodhart). The gold-vs-optimisation curve has **different functional forms for best-of-n vs RL**, with coefficients that scale smoothly with reward-model size. Reward-model dataset size and the KL penalty are key factors — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/gao23h); [OpenAI summary](https://openai.com/index/scaling-laws-for-reward-model-overoptimization/)
- **Reward-model ensembles** (Coste et al., ICLR 2024): ensembles combined by mean, **worst-case (WCO)** or **uncertainty-weighted (UWO)** optimisation "practically eliminate overoptimization and improve performance by up to 70% for best-of-n sampling". For PPO, conservative ensembles plus a small KL penalty prevent overoptimisation "at no performance cost" **(2024–2026)** — [ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/dda7f9378a210c25e470e19304cce85d-Abstract-Conference.html)
- **BOND** (Sessa et al., ICLR 2025): distills the **best-of-N distribution** into the policy by distribution matching with a Jeffreys divergence (mix of forward and reverse KL, to balance mode-covering and mode-seeking), using an iterative moving anchor. This removes the N× inference cost **(2024–2026)** — [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/947f37882a394140f7add476bb99d1d3-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv 2407.14622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14622v1)
- **Advantage-Weighted Regression** (Peng et al. 2019) is RL made of two supervised steps: fit a value baseline, then **regress onto actions weighted by exponentiated advantage**. It handles continuous and discrete actions and fully off-policy data, and refines reward-weighted regression — [arXiv 1910.00177](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.00177v2). A 2025 extension applies online reward-weighted fine-tuning to flow-matching models with Wasserstein regularisation **(2024–2026)** — [arXiv 2502.06061](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.06061) (title/abstract only)
- **Probabilistic-hypothesis + scorer architecture:** ScoreHypo (CVPR 2024) generates multiple hypotheses (HypoNet, diffusion-style) and selects with a learned scorer (ScoreNet). This is the human-mesh version of "generator + reward model + best-of-N" **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Xu_ScoreHypo_Probabilistic_Human_Mesh_Estimation_with_Hypothesis_Scoring_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)
- **Using a limited labelling budget:** active preference learning chooses queries by uncertainty, typically **ensemble disagreement**. PreferenceEKF does sequential Bayesian inference in a low-dimensional subspace **(2024–2026)** — [arXiv 2609.04066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04066). ActiveUltraFeedback "match[es] or outperform[s] previous methods using only one-third of the data" **(2024–2026)** — [arXiv 2603.09692](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.09692v1). Batch active preference learning is NP-hard but approximable as submodular optimisation — [arXiv 1810.04303](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.04303)
- **AI judges are biased:** in MLLM-as-a-Judge (ICML 2024), MLLMs are closest to humans on **pair comparison** but diverge on scoring and batch ranking. They show **position bias** (e.g. LLaVA reproduced the order "ABCD" in 88.2% of batch-ranking answers) and GPT-4V shows **self-preference / egocentric bias** — [arXiv 2402.04788](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04788); [PMLR v235](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/chen24h.html). 2026 work targets "perceptual judgment bias" in MLLM judges **(2024–2026)** — [arXiv 2606.02578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.02578) (title only)

### Inferences
- **Reward model design (a few thousand votes):** do not fine-tune a big backbone. Use frozen features of (photo, render) pairs: ID-embedding cosine, landmark-ratio differences, CLIP/DINO embeddings of both and their difference, optional DNA features. Fit a **Bradley–Terry** head (logistic on the score difference) as an **ensemble of 5–10** (bootstraps or different seeds/feature subsets). Validate pairwise accuracy by identity-grouped CV. Calibrate against the expert-preset hold-out: expert-preset renders should beat student renders most of the time.
- **Before GPT access ends:** (a) spend the remaining budget **actively**, on pairs where the ensemble disagrees most and on demographic slices with few votes; (b) query **both orders** (A/B and B/A) to cancel position bias and drop inconsistent votes; (c) include "anchor" pairs (expert preset vs random DNA, expert vs teacher) for sanity; (d) store every raw vote with the prompt version. Claude remains available as a judge, but self-preference applies if Claude also generated outputs. Here the judged items are renders, so that risk is low.
- **Uses in order of risk:**
  1. **Best-of-N re-ranking at inference** with a *conservative* score: mean − k·std across the ensemble (UWO-like) or the ensemble minimum (WCO), plus ID similarity and the prior log-likelihood as extra terms. Keep N moderate (≈16–64). Gao shows gold reward first rises then falls with optimisation pressure.
  2. **Best-of-N distillation / reward-ranked fine-tuning**: for each training photo, sample N candidates from the student, keep the top-1 (or weight all by exp(r/β), AWR-style), and regress the student onto them. This needs only supervised learning (simple on one GPU) and is the parameter-space analogue of BOND/RAFT. Iterate 2–3 rounds with a human-checked hold-out after each.
  3. **DPO on parameter outputs**: needs a likelihood. With an **MDN or conditional flow head**, log π(DNA|photo) is exact, so the standard DPO loss on (winner DNA, loser DNA) pairs with a frozen reference copy is directly implementable. With a diffusion head, use the Diffusion-DPO ELBO form. Existing votes compare *renders of DNAs* for the same photo, so they map onto (y_w, y_l) pairs. This is my mapping, not taken from a paper.
- **Reward-hacking guards on a small verification budget:**
  - The ensemble with a conservative aggregate (Coste) is the single most effective cheap measure.
  - Add a KL/L2 anchor to the pre-preference student, or limit the number of reward-weighted rounds.
  - Add the plausibility prior so exotic DNA that "fools" CLIP features is penalised.
  - Monitor drift statistics: mean DNA, per-gene variance, attractiveness-proxy scores (if the reward drifts toward attractiveness, that is beautification hacking).
  - Keep a small human-verified panel (the expert-preset identities, plus ~30 ordinary-face photos rated by the user) as the "gold" reward, checked every round.
- **Votes can also train the student directly as a pairwise ranking loss**, without an explicit RM. With only a few thousand pairs, an explicit RM is more reusable: it ranks unlimited new candidates and so amplifies the data.

### Gaps
- No found work applies DPO/RLHF to **low-dimensional parameter generators for avatars**. The mapping above is by analogy (Diffusion-DPO, AWR, BOND).
- Could not verify details of DDPO (Black et al.), D3PO (direct preference for diffusion without a reward model), DPO (Rafailov et al.), KTO or RAFT this session **[background knowledge, not re-verified]**. They are standard references for "RL/preference fine-tuning of non-language generators" and the report writer may cite them cautiously.
- No published guidance quantifies how many pairwise votes a Bradley–Terry RM needs at this feature dimensionality. A few thousand is generally adequate for a low-capacity head (judgement, unsourced).

## 5. Modelling multi-modal outputs and mixed continuous/discrete choices (~100-D, a few thousand real samples)

### Takeaway
Plain L2 regression returns the conditional mean, which averages valid alternatives and pushes toward an average face. For ~100-D DNA with plentiful synthetic data and a few thousand real or teacher samples, the practical order is:
1. **MDN or K-hypothesis heads (winner-takes-all)** for continuous genes, plus **categorical heads** for discrete genes. These are cheap, give an exact likelihood (good for DPO and best-of-N), and work with small data.
2. A **conditional flow (ProHMR-style)**.
3. A **conditional diffusion/flow-matching model over DNA (ScoreHMR/TabDDPM-style)** once synthetic plus pseudo-labelled volume reaches the tens or hundreds of thousands.

For discrete assets, AgileAvatar's **relaxation-and-search** (enumerate each attribute's options with others fixed) is directly usable because CK3 renders are cheap.

### Cited Findings
- **ProHMR** (ICCV 2021) recasts human mesh recovery as learning a *conditional distribution* of plausible parameters with **normalizing flows**, chosen for expressiveness — [arXiv 2108.11944](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.11944)
- **ScoreHMR** (CVPR 2024) learns a **diffusion model over model parameters** conditioned on the image and guides it with task scores at test time **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Stathopoulos_Score-Guided_Diffusion_for_3D_Human_Recovery_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf). **ScoreHypo** (CVPR 2024) produces diverse hypotheses and then scores and selects them **(2024–2026)** — [CVPR 2024 PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Xu_ScoreHypo_Probabilistic_Human_Mesh_Estimation_with_Hypothesis_Scoring_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)
- **TabDDPM** (ICML 2023) handles **mixed numerical and categorical** columns in one model. It uses **Gaussian diffusion for numerical features** (after a Gaussian quantile transform) and **multinomial diffusion for each categorical feature** (one-hot), with an **MLP** denoiser. The loss is MSE plus per-categorical KL terms, the categorical KL is divided by the number of categorical features, and the paper explicitly targets heterogeneous, **small** tabular datasets — [arXiv 2209.15421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.15421); [Yandex Research blog](https://research.yandex.com/blog/tabddpm-modelling-tabular-data-with-diffusion-models)
- **AgileAvatar's discrete handling**: relax discrete parameters, optimise them with the continuous ones through the imitator, then "discretize the relaxed avatar vector by searching over all candidates from the asset list for each attribute while fixing all other parameters" — [arXiv 2211.07818](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.07818)
- NetEase's game has 208 continuous plus several discrete style parameters, so the mixed structure is standard in this domain — [DeepAI: T2P](https://deepai.org/publication/zero-shot-text-to-parameter-translation-for-game-character-auto-creation)
- **Priors for multi-modal parameter spaces**: SMPLify's GMM was chosen "to represent the multi-modal nature of the data while keeping the prior computationally tractable", and VPoser (VAE) gave a stronger prior — [EmergentMind on SMPLify](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/smplify); [arXiv 1904.05866](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.05866)

### Inferences
- **Recommended head design for CK3 DNA:**
  - Continuous genes (~40–80): MDN with K ≈ 4–8 components, diagonal or low-rank covariance, predicted from a shared trunk. Alternatively K deterministic hypotheses trained with a **winner-takes-all / relaxed-WTA loss** plus a small classifier predicting which hypothesis is best. Multiple-hypothesis learning (Rupprecht et al., ICCV 2017) and MDNs (Bishop 1994) are the classic references **[background knowledge, not re-verified]**.
  - Discrete genes (hairstyle, beard, brow shape, etc.): independent **softmax heads** with label-smoothed cross-entropy. Top-k probabilities supply candidates for best-of-N. Gumbel-softmax (Jang et al., ICLR 2017) **[background knowledge, not re-verified]** is only needed if gradients must flow from a differentiable surrogate loss through discrete choices (Fast-F2P-style self-supervision). Otherwise **enumerate**: at ~60 ms per engine render, scoring 20–40 hair options with all else fixed takes about 1–2 s, which is AgileAvatar's search done with the real engine.
  - Colour genes: CK3 stores hair, skin and eye colour as positions in 2D palette textures (my understanding of the format; verify). Regress them as continuous 2D coordinates, possibly with an MDN, instead of classes.
  - Decal genes (wrinkles, freckles, etc.): usually low-dimensional intensities; regress them, gated by a binary "present" head.
- **Why not start with diffusion:** expert and teacher real data are only a few thousand samples, so a diffusion model conditioned on photo features would mostly learn from synthetic pairs. That is fine if synthetic volume is large (100k+). TabDDPM shows MLP diffusion handles mixed types, and ScoreHMR shows a diffusion prior can double as a refinement optimiser. But it adds inference steps and tuning; MDN/WTA heads get most of the multi-modality benefit in a day's work. Phase-2 option: an **unconditional diffusion/flow prior over DNA** trained on synthetic plus human presets, used ScoreHMR-style as a plausibility prior and refiner.
- **Selecting among modes:** use the best-of-N re-ranker from §4 (ID similarity + reward ensemble + prior). Return the top 1 and optionally offer the top 3 as user alternatives. That is also how expert presets differ: different makers choose different but valid modes.
- **Loss for multiple expert presets per identity:** min-over-targets (WTA) or mixture likelihood treats each preset as a valid mode, consistent with §3.

### Gaps
- I found no head-to-head comparison of MDN vs flow vs diffusion for ~100-D conditional parameter regression with a few thousand real samples. The ranking above is reasoned, not sourced.
- I could not verify CK3's exact DNA encoding of colour or decal genes this session; check the CK3 modding wiki or game files.

## 6. Bias control: avoiding "beautification" / average-face drift and balancing demographics

### Takeaway
Beautification has at least four sources:
1. **Regression to the mean.** L2 regression and averaging produce average faces, and average faces are judged more attractive (Langlois & Roggman).
2. **Celebrity-derived labels** (attractive subjects, flattering preset makers).
3. **Biased priors and judges** (AI judges have systematic biases; a reward model may learn "attractive = good").
4. **Demographic imbalance** in source photo sets (FFHQ skews white and young; generative models amplify dataset bias).

Fix each separately: multi-modal heads plus variance calibration for 1; FFHQ teacher labels and residual-magnitude limits for 2; ensemble reward models with drift monitoring and likeness-only judging prompts for 3; stratified sampling or reweighting (FairFace-style balance targets) with per-group evaluation for 4.

### Cited Findings
- **Averageness ≈ attractiveness**: Langlois & Roggman (1990) digitised 192 young white faces and made 2-, 4-, 8-, 16- and 32-face composites. Rated by 300 judges, composites grew more attractive as more faces were averaged, and the 32-face composite was most attractive. The effect replicates across averaging techniques — [Wikipedia: Averageness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averageness); ["Attractive Faces Are Only Average", USU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/fchd_facpub/237)
- **Dataset demographic skew**: FairFace (108,501 images) balances 7 race groups (White, Black, Indian, East Asian, Southeast Asian, Middle Eastern, Latino) and labels gender and 9 age groups, though it is "not fully balanced" across those. It was built because public face datasets are "strongly biased toward Caucasian faces" — [FairFace, arXiv 1908.04913](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04913); [summary](https://tldr.takara.ai/p/1908.04913). An aggregator states that most face datasets are "often 70–80% white" with male skew (secondary source, treat as approximate) — [VerifyWise](https://verifywise.ai/ai-governance-library/datasets-and-benchmarks/fairface-dataset)
- **Generative models amplify dataset bias**: unconditional diffusion models "accentuate the existing bias in datasets with respect to age, gender and race" (FFHQ 70k context) — [Gaussian Harmony, arXiv 2312.14976](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.14976)
- **AI-judge biases**: position bias and self-preference in MLLM judges; pair comparison is the judge format most aligned with humans — [MLLM-as-a-Judge, arXiv 2402.04788](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04788)
- **Balanced subset construction in a game-face paper**: MeInGame used equal ethnicity × gender subsets (6 × 400) — [arXiv 2102.02371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.02371)
- **Over-optimised reward proxies drift from the true goal** (Goodhart) — [Gao et al., ICML 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/gao23h)

### Inferences
- **Regression-to-mean fixes (the biggest lever):**
  - Use multi-modal heads (§5) instead of a single L2 mean.
  - Apply **per-gene variance/slope calibration**: on held-out data, regress true DNA on predicted DNA. A slope > 1 means the model is shrinking, so rescale deviations from the population mean by the fitted factor, i.e. a learned "caricature" gain.
  - Monitor the **distribution of predicted DNA** against the distribution of real people's DNA (from expert presets and teacher labels on FFHQ). Predicted per-gene variance should not collapse.
  - Include "distinctiveness" checks: nearest-neighbour ID-similarity between different people's outputs should not be higher than between their photos.
- **Celebrity-label bias:** use celebrity expert pairs mainly for calibration and evaluation, keep the residual model low-capacity, and **cap residual magnitude**. Teacher labels on **ordinary FFHQ faces** carry the main real-photo signal. Check whether residuals correlate with an attractiveness proxy; if they systematically push toward "prettier" (e.g. symmetric, slimmer, younger), drop or regularise those components.
- **Judge and reward bias:** phrase judge prompts as "which render is the *same person*" (likeness), explicitly not "which looks better". Randomise left/right and query both orders. Include pairs where the *less attractive* render is the correct likeness (e.g. the expert preset of an older or heavy-set person against a generic young face) to test whether the judge or RM is attractiveness-driven. Track the RM's correlation with an attractiveness proxy as a hacking alarm.
- **Demographic balancing:**
  - Run an age/gender/ethnicity classifier on FFHQ, then **stratify** the teacher-labelled subset to near-equal cells (FairFace-style targets, plus extra elderly and heavy-set faces).
  - Reweight the loss by inverse cell frequency.
  - Sample synthetic DNA to cover the full range of age, skin and weight genes, not just the vanilla-character distribution.
  - Report all metrics **per demographic cell**, and treat worst-group judge score as a release criterion.
- **Synthetic-prior bias:** if synthetic DNA is sampled from a GMM fitted to celebrity presets, the student's prior inherits celebrity bias. Use a broad sampling mixture (§1).

#### Cross-cutting synthesis: a concrete recipe for the CK3 project on one RTX 5080 (inference from all sections)
1. **Features:** cache frozen-extractor features (dense landmarks/ratios, parsing, face-ID, CLIP/DINO) for renders and photos. Training then only fits heads (minutes to hours).
2. **Synthetic stage:** about 300k–1M renders (one or two nights) with broad DNA sampling and heavy augmentation. Train a trunk with MDN/WTA continuous heads, softmax discrete heads and 2D-colour heads. Also train a **DNA → render-features surrogate** for loopback loss, IEF and fast refinement.
3. **Teacher stage:** run the slow pipeline on 3–5k demographically stratified FFHQ photos, warm-started by the student (SPIN loop). Filter by quality and train with confidence weights. Repeat once (CameraHMR-style).
4. **Expert stage:** hold out about 35 identities. On the rest, fit per-gene calibration plus a ridge/GP residual with identity-grouped CV. Use inter-preset spread for per-gene weights and as the human-level bar. Fit the plausibility prior on all human and vanilla DNA.
5. **Preference stage:** train a Bradley–Terry RM ensemble on the votes (active-query the remaining GPT budget, both orders). At inference, run conservative best-of-N with N ≈ 16–48 real renders in under 5 s (assumes ~60 ms/render). Offline, do 1–3 rounds of best-of-N / reward-weighted distillation into the student, checked on a human-verified panel.
6. **Bias checks every round:** predicted-DNA variance vs reference, attractiveness-proxy drift, worst-demographic-group score, cross-photo consistency.
